1
Species Recovery - Shorebirds

A collaborative 15-year effort spanning 35 coastal organizations across 16 states has driven a 45% population increase in American oystercatchers along the Atlantic and Gulf coasts, reversing decades of decline from hunting and habitat loss. The conservation strategy combined beachgoer education, predator management on nesting sites, and focused habitat protection across refuges like Trustom Pond in Rhode Island. Scientists attribute the success to chick survival improvements and note that similar recovery has not been achieved for most shorebirds in North America, making this a exceptional conservation win. However, recent changes to the Endangered Species Act threaten to undermine protections for critical nesting habitats.

2
US Environmental Policy

Despite the Trump administration's sweeping changes to the federal Endangered Species Act, Oregon's state forest regulators are moving forward with habitat conservation plans to protect critical ecosystems for threatened species like marbled murrelets and spotted owls. The Oregon Board of Forestry approved a new management plan that restricts logging and protects sensitive habitats on half of the state's 640,000 acres of forest land, reflecting Oregonians' overwhelming support for fish and wildlife protections. Conservation groups worry that federal changes redefining "harm" under the act could eliminate critical habitat protections, but state leaders are determined to continue their long-term conservation work. The decision underscores how states are stepping up when federal protections weaken.

3
Indigenous Stewardship - Canada

The Kaska First Nations in partnership with the Dena Keyah Institute have unveiled the draft plan for Dene K'éh Kusãn ("always will be there" in Kaska), a massive Indigenous Protected and Conserved Area spanning over 30,000 square kilometers of northern British Columbia wilderness—larger than the entire country of Belgium. The initiative represents a paradigm shift in land management, allowing the Kaska to lead protection efforts based on Indigenous laws, governance, and stewardship traditions while balancing conservation with sustainable economic activities. After months of consultation, the project opened for public feedback through August 4, with approval expected before implementation. This approach is part of a growing movement across Canada, with dozens of similar IPCA projects underway.

4
Wetland Restoration - Massachusetts

The former Pine Grove Golf Course in Northampton, Massachusetts is undergoing a groundbreaking $2 million restoration to become a self-sustaining, climate-resilient wetland—the first large-scale project of its kind in the state. Volunteers and restoration ecologists removed artificial drainage infrastructure, constructed beaver dam analogs, and replanted native species to restore natural stream flow; beavers have already established their own 8-foot dam on site, attracting herons and swallows. The project, led by the Massachusetts Division of Ecological Restoration and Mass Audubon, is designed to serve as a model for the hundreds of golf courses closing nationwide. Phase 2, expected to cost $3 million, will fully restore the Nashawannuck Brook's natural course and create nearly two miles of accessible trails.

5
Marine Conservation - Global

A landmark study published in Science reveals that mangrove forests worldwide are experiencing a remarkable comeback, with global losses slowing dramatically and gains increasing for the first time in decades—challenging decades of assumptions about their vulnerability. Researchers using satellite imagery from 1980-2023 found that natural regeneration and expansion are now offsetting much historical decline, with restoration efforts in Kenya, Costa Rica, Benin, and Southeast Asia demonstrating how science-based conservation and community stewardship deliver results. Mangrove ecosystems, comprising less than 1% of tropical forests, support over 1,500 species and shield 2.4 billion coastal residents from storms and erosion while storing massive amounts of carbon. The UN Environment Programme emphasizes that success depends on sustained investment and long-term protection, noting that satellite data alone cannot reveal threats like pollution and biodiversity loss.

6
Deforestation - International

A new analysis published in Science warns that the abandonment of the Amazon Soy Moratorium—which had successfully reduced soy-linked deforestation to near-zero since 2006—will trigger at least 1.4 million hectares of additional deforestation across Brazil by 2036, releasing carbon equivalent to Canada's annual emissions. The moratorium, under attack for three years from soy farmers and regional governments, effectively collapsed in January after state laws stripped tax incentives from companies adopting eco-friendly sourcing. The research team projects that this forest loss would represent roughly 17% of all deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon over the past decade, seriously undermining progress since President Lula's return to power in 2023. Scientists recommend stronger enforcement by Brazil and more ethical sourcing demands from EU and Chinese soy importers to prevent catastrophic habitat destruction.

7
Environmental Justice - US Policy

A South Carolina federal judge ordered the Environmental Protection Agency to immediately resume distributing the $2.8 billion Environmental and Climate Justice Block Grant Program, rejecting the Trump administration's attempts to evade a June ruling that found terminations "unlawful." The program, created under the Biden-era Inflation Reduction Act, was designed to help underserved communities address air pollution, extreme heat, and infrastructure challenges—with grants like $500,000 intended for CleanAIRE NC to install air quality monitors in pollution-exposed areas. Judge Richard Gergel emphasized that the EPA must comply with its legal obligations through September 2026, the original expiration date, making clear that federal agencies cannot ignore court orders. The decision underscores ongoing legal battles over environmental justice funding and demonstrates courts' role in protecting climate and environmental programs.

8
International Conservation - Ecuador

Ecuador announced the creation of the Yasuní-Limoncocha-Cuyabeno Corridor, a 173,000-hectare (427,000-acre) conservation corridor that reconnects three isolated protected areas in the Amazon, supported by Conservation International, World Wildlife Fund, and the Global Environment Facility. The corridor bridges gaps created by oil development and agricultural expansion, allowing wildlife like jaguars, tapirs, and monkeys to move freely across fragmented habitat while supporting sustainable livelihoods for the Kichwa, Siona, and Siekopai peoples who have stewarded the land for generations. Unlike traditional protected areas that exclude human activity, this corridor is designed to support wildlife and people together, with Indigenous communities leading management alongside government and NGO partners. The model exemplifies a global shift toward conservation that recognizes local ecological knowledge and community rights as central to protecting biodiversity.
