1
Penguins and Dolphins Threatened by 'Catastrophic Decline' from Bird Flu

Nearly 400 Australian species, including penguins, dolphins, and other marine life, face serious threats from an ongoing bird flu outbreak affecting the region. Scientists fear a 'catastrophic decline' in populations following recent surveys showing drastically lower numbers than expected. The outbreak highlights how wildlife disease can rapidly spread across ecosystems and devastate vulnerable populations already pressured by habitat loss and climate change.

2
Global Marine Protected Areas Fail to Safeguard Sharks and Rays, Study Finds

A new study reveals that most marine protected areas (MPAs) worldwide are inadequate at safeguarding sharks and rays from overfishing and habitat degradation. Despite representing a key conservation tool, many MPAs lack the enforcement and size needed to protect these vulnerable predators that play crucial roles in ocean ecosystems. The findings underscore the urgent need for stronger ocean governance and more comprehensive protection strategies to prevent further decline in elasmobranch populations.

3
Trump Administration Further Weakens Endangered Species Act Protections

The Trump administration has redefined key terms in the Endangered Species Act, removing critical protections that shield imperiled wildlife from habitat destruction and development. The changes threaten vulnerable species including Vermont's Canadian lynx, Jesup's Milk-vetch, and countless others already struggling to survive. Advocates warn that weakening the ESA—America's most powerful conservation law—jeopardizes 50 years of species recovery efforts.

4
Western Spadefoot Toad Gains Protected Status Under California Law

The California Fish and Game Commission has designated the western spadefoot toad as a candidate species under the California Endangered Species Act (CESA), granting it critical take protections that prevent harm and harassment. This rare desert amphibian faces mounting pressures from habitat conversion, drought, and development in the southwestern United States. The designation represents an important step toward preventing the species' slide toward extinction.

5
Protesters Occupy Historic Cottonwood Tree at US-Mexico Border to Stop Its Destruction

Indigenous and environmental activists have occupied a 200-year-old cottonwood tree at the US-Mexico border in Arizona, calling it their 'Statue of Liberty' and refusing to allow border wall construction to fell it. The tree is a vital habitat for wildlife, a cultural landmark, and a symbol of the borderlands' natural heritage. The standoff highlights the tension between infrastructure projects and conservation of irreplaceable ecosystems and cultural resources.

6
Ancient Forests in Amazon See Renewed Hope as Global Deforestation Rates Climb

While global deforestation pressures continue to mount, new satellite monitoring reveals that protected Indigenous territories and conservation reserves in the Amazon are successfully maintaining forest cover and resisting clearing. These victories demonstrate that when Indigenous communities are given secure land rights and resources, they become some of the world's most effective forest guardians. The data underscores the critical importance of supporting Indigenous land stewardship as a key strategy in the fight against deforestation.

7
Water Quality and Wildlife Recovery Drive Investment in Landscape Restoration

Conservation organizations across North America are scaling up landscape restoration projects that simultaneously improve water quality, restore wildlife habitat, and build climate resilience. These initiatives recognize that ecosystem health is interconnected—restoring forests, wetlands, and grasslands benefits not only wildlife but also human communities that depend on clean water and stable climate. The momentum suggests growing recognition that nature-based solutions offer multiple benefits compared to purely engineered approaches.

8
Indigenous Land Restitution in South America Proves Conservation Success

Research from Chile, Argentina, and other South American nations shows that returning land to Indigenous communities results in measurably better conservation outcomes. Studies tracking restitution programs document increased habitat protection, reduced invasive species, and stronger ecosystem resilience when Indigenous peoples reclaim their ancestral territories. These successes reinforce that supporting Indigenous self-determination is not just a matter of justice—it's one of the most effective conservation strategies available.
