State Conservation Policy - US

Oregon Board Approves Forest Management Plan Protecting Endangered Species Despite Federal Changes

The Oregon Board of Forestry approved a landmark management plan on Wednesday for the state's 640,000 acres of state forests, committing to restrict logging and protect sensitive habitats on half the forest area while allowing sustainable timber use on the other half. The plan was approved despite recent Trump administration changes to the federal Endangered Species Act that remove habitat protections, demonstrating Oregon's commitment to safeguarding species like marbled murrelets and spotted owls. State leaders emphasized that habitat protection remains essential for endangered species recovery, warning that federal changes could undermine decades of collaborative conservation policymaking involving landowners, conservationists, and government agencies.

Endangered Species - US Policy

New Federal Rules Weaken Protections for More Than 100 Florida Species Including Panthers and Manatees

The Trump administration's new regulations on the Endangered Species Act, effective this month, threaten over 100 Florida species by removing habitat degradation from the definition of "harm"—meaning projects can destroy critical wildlife habitats as long as they don't directly kill animals. Florida manatees, facing starvation from pollution-caused algae blooms that killed 1,255 animals in the Indian River Lagoon between 2020-2022, and Florida panthers, whose habitat faces rapid development, are among the most vulnerable species. Conservation groups warn the changes will accelerate coastal development, weaken tourism-dependent ecosystems, and silence environmental advocates who have relied on habitat protection lawsuits for decades. The Florida Wildlife Federation calls Florida "ground zero" for these changes, given the state's rapid development and irreplaceable wildlife habitat.

Marine & Coastal Ecosystem Recovery - International

Global Mangrove Forests Stage Remarkable Recovery After Decades of Decline

Scientists analyzing four decades of satellite imagery from 1980-2023 have documented a striking turnaround for the world's mangrove forests—once disappearing at alarming rates due to aquaculture, agriculture, and coastal development, mangrove losses have now plateaued and natural regrowth is reversing centuries of decline. Kenya's mangrove restoration programs have expanded to protect 60,000 hectares, while Costa Rican conservation groups have replanted over 12,000 native mangrove seedlings with 80% survival rates, creating employment for fishing communities. Mangrove ecosystems are climate superheroes, storing multiple times more carbon per hectare than terrestrial forests while providing nurseries for fish, protection from storms, and livelihoods for 2.4 billion people living within 100 kilometers of coastlines.

Coral Reef Restoration - Marine

Iconic Reefs Initiative Aims to Restore Five Million Corals in Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary

NOAA's Iconic Reefs initiative has launched an ambitious 20-year coral restoration program targeting five million corals across the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary, combining ocean-based and land-based nurseries to build climate-resilient reef systems. The initiative is training Native Hawaiians and community members in coral restoration and scientific diving, creating pathways to marine science careers while addressing the ecological and economic crisis of reef decline. Coral reefs protect hundreds of thousands of marine species, support global fisheries that feed millions, and defend coastlines from storms—restoration efforts recognize that protecting these ecosystems means ensuring the survival of human communities equally dependent on them.

Tropical Forest & Deforestation - Climate Solution

Madagascar's Rainforest Conservation Effort Expands to Protect Endemic Wildlife and Store Carbon

Madagascar's endemic biodiversity—90% of the island's species exist nowhere else on Earth—faces mounting pressure from deforestation, yet conservation groups are expanding protected areas and working with local communities to restore degraded forest patches. Lemur populations, iconic symbols of Madagascar's unique evolutionary heritage, depend on rapidly vanishing forest habitat, and restoration programs are creating corridors that allow animals to move safely between protected reserves. Forests, particularly in tropical regions, store more carbon per hectare than any other ecosystem and provide irreplaceable habitat for species found nowhere else—protecting them is both a climate imperative and a uniqueness-preservation mission for some of Earth's rarest animals.

Community-Based Conservation - Indigenous Stewardship

Indigenous Communities Expand Traditional Land Stewardship Programs Across North America

Indigenous nations across North America are gaining recognition and funding for traditional ecological stewardship practices that enhance biodiversity, manage fire risk, and sequester carbon—challenging the colonial model of "wilderness" preservation without communities. Tribal land management, refined over thousands of years, demonstrates that human communities and thriving ecosystems can coexist when Indigenous knowledge guides conservation decisions. Conservation organizations are increasingly partnering with Indigenous-led initiatives, recognizing that Indigenous-managed lands show superior biodiversity and carbon storage outcomes compared to parks managed without traditional stewardship, and that supporting Indigenous sovereignty is fundamental to planetary health.

Ocean Acidification & Marine Research - Science

New Deep-Sea Coral Research Reveals How Ocean Ecosystems Adapt to Changing Chemistry

NOAA researchers studying Maine's deep-sea coral communities have discovered surprising resilience mechanisms in coral species living in cold, high-pressure environments facing rapid chemical changes in ocean water. The research provides critical insights into how marine ecosystems respond to ocean acidification caused by rising CO₂ absorption, informing conservation strategies for species that have survived dramatic environmental shifts before. Understanding deep-sea coral adaptation is vital because these ecosystems support unique communities of animals and may hold clues to how other marine life could adapt to a rapidly warming ocean.
